01 Starting the work

Writing sharper briefs

The quality of a brief determines the quality of everything downstream. Use Claude to pressure-test vague thinking, surface missing decisions, and write briefs that teams can actually act on.

Turn a vague ask into a brief
I have a rough design ask. Turn it into a proper creative brief with: a clear objective, target audience, tone of voice, deliverables, and three questions I should answer before work starts. Here's the ask: [paste client ask or your own notes]

Why it works: You're not asking Claude to write the brief for you — you're feeding it your own thinking and asking it to structure and interrogate it. The output belongs to you.

Identify what's missing
Read this brief and tell me: what decisions haven't been made yet, what assumptions are being made about the audience, and what could cause misalignment between designer and client? Brief: [paste brief]

Why it works: Claude acts as a second reader who isn't invested in the brief — it'll surface the gaps you're too close to see.

Write a challenger brief
Here's my current brief. Write a "challenger version" — same objective, but with a more ambitious or unexpected strategic angle. Don't redesign the whole thing, just push the thinking one level further. Brief: [paste brief]

Why it works: Great for when a brief feels safe or obvious — Claude will find a different angle you can choose to take or not.

Pro tip: Brief as conversation starter

Paste the brief at the start of a conversation and say "Keep this in mind for everything in this chat." Claude will use it as context for all follow-up questions — you won't need to repeat yourself.

02 Language as design

Naming & copy that sounds human

Brand names, taglines, headlines, packaging copy — the words in a design are design. Claude won't replace your copywriter, but it will give you 40 options by the time your copywriter gets back to you.

Brand name generation
Generate 20 brand name options for [describe brand/product]. Organise them into four columns: — Descriptive (says what it does) — Evocative (feels like the brand) — Abstract (invented or unexpected) — Real words, reframed (familiar word, new context) Audience: [who they are] Tone: [3 adjectives] Avoid anything that sounds like: [competitors or clichés to avoid]

Why it works: Structuring into categories forces a range instead of a list of similar-sounding options. The "avoid" field is crucial — without it, Claude defaults to safe territory.

Tagline variations
Write 10 tagline options for [brand name + one sentence about what it does]. For each one, write it in three tones: confident, warm, and witty. I want options, not one direction. The brand truth is: [the one thing that makes this brand different]

Why it works: Asking for three tones per idea stops Claude from committing to a single voice before you've decided what the brand sounds like.

Rewrite for a specific voice
Rewrite this copy to match the brand voice below. Don't change the meaning — change how it sounds. Original copy: [paste copy] Brand voice: [e.g. "direct and dry, like a smart friend who doesn't waste words. No exclamation marks. No 'we're passionate about'. Short sentences."]

Why it works: The more specific your voice description, the more specific the output. Describe the voice with examples of what to avoid, not just what to aim for.

Vague prompt
Write me some taglines for a skincare brand.
Specific prompt
Write 8 taglines for a gender-neutral skincare brand targeting 28–40 year olds who are skeptical of wellness trends. Tone: understated, scientific, a little dry. The brand truth: "formulated to actually work, not to look good on a shelf." Avoid: words like "glow", "ritual", "journey", "clean".

03 Visual direction

Moodboarding & visual direction

Claude can't generate images — but it can describe visual worlds so precisely that you know exactly what to search for, and why. Use it to articulate a direction before you start collecting references.

Describe a visual world
Describe the visual world of a brand with these characteristics: [paste brand description or brief] Include: — Colour palette (describe colours in sensory terms, not hex) — Texture and material quality (e.g. "matte, uncoated paper" or "gloss with emboss") — Typography personality (not font names — describe the feeling) — Photography or illustration style — Three real-world environments where this brand would feel at home — One brand or designer whose work has a similar visual DNA (not a competitor)

Why it works: Sensory and metaphorical descriptions give you better search terms for Pinterest, Are.na, or stock photography than asking for technical specs.

Name three directions
Give me three distinct visual directions for [project name/type]. Each should be genuinely different — not just variations on the same mood. For each direction: — A two-word name that captures the feeling (not a generic label) — A one-paragraph visual description — Three search terms I could use on a reference site — What this direction says about the brand that the others don't

Why it works: Naming the directions forces differentiation. "Modern Minimal" and "Clean Contemporary" are the same direction — a two-word name with character forces real distinction.

Use Claude to write your moodboard rationale

Found your references? Describe them to Claude and ask it to write the rationale: "Here are 5 images I've selected: [describe each]. Write a paragraph that explains what they have in common and why this visual direction is right for the brand." Client presentations just got easier.

IDEAS
04 Generative thinking

Concept ideation & exploration

Claude thinks fast and wide. Use it at the beginning of a project to explore territory before committing, or when you're stuck and need a way out of the obvious answer.

Break from the obvious
I'm designing [project] for [brand/client]. The obvious approach would be [describe the safe/expected solution]. Give me 6 concepts that subvert that expectation. Each should be genuinely surprising — not just the obvious approach with a twist. For each: a one-line concept description and a concrete visual execution idea.

Why it works: Naming the obvious answer first is the key move — it tells Claude what territory to leave behind, forcing genuinely unexpected outputs.

Steal from another world
I need ideas for [project type, e.g. "a brand identity for a law firm"]. Instead of looking at the design category it normally lives in, I want you to pull inspiration from these unrelated worlds: [e.g. "Japanese street fashion, 1970s scientific illustration, deep-sea navigation charts"] For each world, describe one concept for how that aesthetic or logic could translate into the brand identity.

Why it works: Cross-domain thinking is where original ideas come from. Claude is very good at drawing structural parallels between unrelated fields.

Push a concept further
Here's a concept I'm developing: [describe your concept] Ask me the 5 questions you would need answered to push this concept to its fullest, most coherent expression. Don't tell me what to do — just ask the questions.

Why it works: When you're mid-concept, you don't need more ideas — you need the right questions. This forces you to develop what you have rather than abandoning it.

Combine two worlds

Ask Claude to merge two unexpected aesthetics: "What would a brutalist approach to luxury packaging look like?"

⏪

Go historical

Ask for design precedents: "What movements or designers have solved a similar visual problem to mine? What can I steal?"

Invert the brief

Ask Claude to design the anti-version: "What would the worst possible interpretation of this brief look like?" Then find the opposite.

Find the constraint

Ask: "What single creative constraint — a rule I must follow — would force the most interesting solution here?"

05Honest capabilities

Claude & image generation

Claude doesn't render photographs or raster art — it's the art director, not the renderer. It writes precise, reusable prompts for Midjourney and Firefly, and draws native SVG and HTML visuals directly in chat.

In this chapter What Claude can / can't renderMidjourney prompt systems4 copy-paste prompts~8 min read

Set expectations first

Claude renders natively

SVG logos, icons and illustrations; HTML/CSS mockups; charts and diagrams. Vector and code-based visuals, editable and yours outright.

Claude can't render

Photographic or photoreal raster imagery. No pixels, no renders. For that, Claude partners with a generator — see the workflow card.

The real workflow

Claude writes and systematises the prompts your generator consumes — consistent style language, fewer wasted generations, on-brand output.

Prompts that make your generator behave

Build a reusable brand prompt system
Build me a reusable Midjourney prompt template for [brand name]. Brand aesthetic: [describe — e.g. "warm editorial photography, natural light, muted earth tones, 35mm feel"] We generate images of: [typical subjects — products, lifestyle scenes, abstract textures] Output: — A base style string I append to every prompt (lighting, lens, palette, mood) — 3 subject "slots" with example phrasing for each — Recommended parameters (aspect ratios per channel, stylize value) — 3 complete example prompts using the system

Why it works: Consistency across a campaign is the hard part of generated imagery. A prompt system makes generation #40 match generation #1.

Translate a brief into 6 visual directions
Here is my creative brief: [paste brief] Write 6 distinct image-generation prompts, each exploring a different visual direction (e.g. documentary photography, flat illustration, 3D render, paper collage, cyanotype, macro texture). For each: name the direction, the full prompt, and one sentence on when this direction would be the right call.

Why it works: You explore wider than your default style before burning generator credits — moodboarding at the prompt level.

Fix a prompt that keeps failing
This image-generation prompt isn't giving me what I want. My prompt: [paste it] What I'm getting: [describe the wrong results] What I actually want: [describe the goal image] Diagnose what's ambiguous or conflicting, rewrite the prompt, and give me negative prompts / exclusions to add.

Why it works: Generators fail on vague or contradictory language. Claude is excellent at spotting exactly which words are sending the model sideways.

Skip the generator — draw it as SVG
Create an SVG illustration of [subject] in a [geometric / single-line / flat / isometric] style. Constraints: — Palette: [your hex values] — Maximum [N] shapes, clean grouped layers — Works at 32px and at 400px Then give me one variation with a different composition.

Why it works: For icons, spot illustrations and diagrams, native SVG beats a raster generator: instantly editable, infinitely scalable, and unambiguously yours.

Pro tip: keep a prompt library per client

Paste winning generator prompts back into that client's Claude Project. The whole team then generates from the same style system — and Claude refines it conversation by conversation.

Before generated imagery ships to a client

  • Generator terms differ: Adobe Firefly positions itself as commercially safe; Midjourney's terms tie ownership to your subscription tier. Read the current terms of whichever tool renders the final asset.
  • Disclose it: put AI-generated imagery in your SOW or deliverables notes — surprise is the real professional risk. Details in the IP & ownership chapter.
CRIT
06 Getting sharper

Design critique & feedback

Describe your design — or better, upload it (see the vision chapter) — and Claude critiques like a sharp creative director. It will always tell you what it thinks, not what you want to hear.

Structural critique
I'm going to describe a design I've created. Act as a senior creative director giving honest feedback. Be critical — I don't need encouragement, I need to make it better. The project: [brief in one sentence] The design: [describe layout, type, colour, imagery, key decisions] What I think is working: [your own assessment] What I'm unsure about: [your doubts]

Why it works: Telling Claude what you think is working and what you're unsure about skips the generic feedback and gets to the questions you actually have.

Ask from the client's perspective
Here's my design: [describe it] The client is: [describe who they are — their role, priorities, what they're nervous about] Roleplay as this client seeing the work for the first time. What's their gut reaction? What questions will they ask? What will they push back on — and what's the underlying concern behind each pushback?

Why it works: Client feedback roleplay is one of the most underused ways to use Claude. Anticipate the objections before you're in the room.

Compare two directions
I have two design directions. Help me think through which one to develop further. Direction A: [describe] Direction B: [describe] The success criteria for this project is: [what does "good" look like here?] Don't just tell me which is better — tell me what each direction does well, what it sacrifices, and what question I should answer before I decide.

Why it works: Asking for what each direction sacrifices — not just what it achieves — is the most useful framing for a genuine strategic decision.

"Claude will give you a real opinion. Ask for one — don't leave it room to hedge."

Upload a comp and get hierarchy, contrast and alignment feedback on what's really there — not on your description of it.

A moodboard, a photo, a competitor's site — Claude pulls the dominant colors as hex values with suggested usage roles.

Copy-paste vision prompts

Structured critique of an uploaded design
[Upload your design, then send:] Critique this design as a senior art director. Context: [what it is, who it's for] Work through: 1. Visual hierarchy — where does the eye go first, second, third? Is that the right order? 2. Typography — scale, pairing, line length issues 3. Color & contrast — flag anything that looks like a WCAG risk 4. Spacing & alignment — anything optically off 5. The ONE change with the most impact Point to specific locations in the image when you flag something.

Why it works: Feedback anchored to real coordinates in a real image beats feedback on a verbal description every time.

Extract a usable palette from any image
[Upload the image, then send:] Extract the 6 dominant colors as hex values. For each: — A name — Its role in a palette (background / text / primary accent / secondary) — One contrast-safe text color to pair it with These are estimates from the image — tell me which values I should verify with an eyedropper.

Why it works: Turns any moodboard or reference photo into a working starting palette in seconds — with honesty built in about verification.

A/B test two directions against the brief
[Upload both directions, then send:] These are two design directions for the same brief. The brief: [one-line goal] The audience: [who] Which direction serves the brief better, and why? Score each 1–10 on: first impression, clarity of hierarchy, brand fit. Then tell me what the winning direction should steal from the losing one. No diplomacy.

Why it works: "Which do you like?" invites taste. Scoring against the brief invites judgment — and the "steal from the loser" step is where the real synthesis happens.

Reverse-engineer a competitor's layout
[Upload a full-page screenshot, then send:] Reverse-engineer this design: — Grid: columns, gutters, margins (best estimate) — Type scale: how many sizes, rough ratio — Spacing rhythm: the repeating unit — Palette and how color is rationed — Conversion patterns: what is this page doing to drive action? End with: 3 things worth learning from (not copying) for [my project].

Why it works: Competitor analysis becomes structural instead of vibes — you take away the system, not the surface.

Pro tip: image quality drives feedback quality

Full-resolution screenshots beat photos of screens. Crop to the area in question for detailed feedback. For packaging or environmental work, upload multiple angles in one message.

Vision honesty box

  • Color values are estimates. Vision reads colors approximately — always verify final hex values with your eyedropper.
  • Tiny type can be misread. If the copy matters, upload a zoomed crop of the text block.
  • Confidential client work: check your privacy settings (or use a business plan) before uploading anything under NDA — full guidance in the IP & ownership chapter.

08 Presenting & persuading

Client communication & presentations

The presentation is part of the design. Use Claude to write rationale, sharpen your narrative, prepare for hard conversations, and translate your creative thinking into language clients respond to.

Write design rationale
Write a presentation rationale for this design decision. It should explain the thinking without over-explaining — confident, not defensive. The decision: [e.g. "we chose a black-and-white palette with a single red accent"] The strategic reason: [why this is right for the brand] The audience for this presentation: [who you're presenting to] Keep it to 3–4 sentences. It should sound like a designer with a point of view, not a committee.

Why it works: "Sound like a designer with a point of view, not a committee" is a constraint that dramatically changes the tone of the output.

Prepare for pushback
I'm presenting this work tomorrow: [describe the design] The client tends to: [e.g. "push for more colour", "ask for a safer option", "compare to competitors"] Give me: 1. The three most likely objections 2. The underlying concern behind each one (what they're really saying) 3. A response that addresses the real concern — not just the surface objection

Why it works: Separating the objection from the underlying concern is the key to responding well. "Can we make it brighter?" often means "I'm worried it won't stand out."

Write a project narrative
Write a short project narrative for a case study / presentation for: [project name] Structure: — The challenge (1–2 sentences) — The insight that unlocked the solution (1–2 sentences) — The approach (2–3 sentences) — The outcome (1 sentence) Tone: direct and confident. No design jargon. No "we leveraged synergies." Key facts to include: [any specifics — awards, metrics, timeline, constraints]

Why it works: The "no design jargon" constraint is essential — without it, Claude defaults to the same buzzwords you're trying to avoid.

09 Understanding the landscape

Research & competitive context

Before you design, you need to understand the space. Claude can quickly map a category's visual conventions, identify what's been done to death, and help you find the gap worth designing into.

Map a category's visual conventions
I'm designing a brand identity for a company in the [industry/category] space. Describe the visual conventions that dominate this category — the colours, type styles, photography approaches, and design clichés that appear everywhere. Be specific. Then tell me: what's the visual space that nobody is occupying?

Why it works: The question "what space is nobody occupying?" is the most strategically useful output from category research. It reframes convention-mapping from descriptive to generative.

Brief yourself on an unfamiliar client
I have a new client in an industry I don't know well: [industry] Help me prepare for our first meeting. Give me: — The 5 things that matter most to buyers in this category — The language and terminology insiders use (and outsiders get wrong) — The two or three shifts happening in this space right now — One question I should ask that will make me look like I understand the business

Why it works: Walking into a client meeting knowing their world — even at a surface level — builds trust before you've shown them a single thing.

Important: Verify anything time-sensitive

Claude's knowledge has a training cutoff. For recent brand launches, campaign news, or fast-moving category trends, use Claude to structure your research questions — then verify the specifics with a current source.

10 Building for scale

Design systems & documentation

The least glamorous part of brand work — and the part clients use every day. Claude is exceptionally good at writing clear, consistent guidelines that other people can actually follow.

Write a usage rule
Write a clear, specific usage rule for this brand guideline element: [e.g. "the primary typeface", "the logo clear space", "the brand photography style"] Include: what to do, what not to do, and one example of each. Write it for someone who knows nothing about design — clear enough that they can't misinterpret it. Firm but not defensive in tone.

Why it works: "Write it for someone who knows nothing about design" is the key constraint — brand guidelines fail when they assume too much knowledge.

Define a voice and tone section
Write a voice and tone section for a brand guidelines document. The brand is: [describe the brand and its personality in a few sentences] Include: — A short description of the brand's voice (not adjective lists — describe it like you'd describe a person) — 4 before/after examples showing the voice in practice — What to avoid (with examples of what that sounds like) The guidelines will be used by: [in-house team / external agencies / social media managers / all of the above]

Why it works: Before/after examples are the most useful thing in a voice guide. Lists of adjectives tell you nothing — "bold" means different things to everyone.

Part A — Setting up a Canva template with Claude

The full walkthrough for building a reusable branded Canva template from scratch, using Claude at every strategic and verbal step. By the end you have a template your team fills in without a designer.

1

Define the template's single job

Before opening Canva, open Claude and get clear on what the template needs to do. One template, one job — this shapes every layout decision downstream.

Prompt: "I want to create a Canva template for [e.g. 'a weekly LinkedIn post announcing product updates']. Tell me: what information needs to be on it every time, what changes each week, and what visual hierarchy would make it scannable in under 3 seconds?" Output: a content map of fixed vs. variable elements
2

Write the copy architecture

Ask Claude to define every text element — its purpose, maximum character count, and tone rule. This becomes your brief for labelling every Canva text box.

Prompt: "For this template, define every text element. For each one give me: field name, purpose, character limit, and tone rule. Example format: — HEADLINE: announces the update | max 8 words | punchy, benefit-led, no verbs ending in -ing — SUBHEADLINE: expands the headline | max 20 words | conversational, one complete sentence — BODY: supporting detail | max 40 words | factual, third-person optional" Output: a structured copy spec — label each Canva text box with this info as placeholder text
3

Build the layout in Canva

Now open Canva and build the template structure. Set up text boxes matching your Claude copy architecture. Use the placeholder text (e.g. "HEADLINE — max 8 words") directly in the text boxes so future users know exactly what goes where.

  • Apply your Brand Kit: lock in colours, fonts, logo
  • Set text styles at the brand level (not per-text-box)
  • Lock elements that should never change: logo, brand bar, legal footer
  • Leave all variable areas fully editable and clearly labelled
In Canva: right-click element → Lock to protect fixed brand elements
4

Write realistic dummy content with Claude

Templates with generic placeholder text get misused because people don't understand the intent. Ask Claude to write realistic, on-brand dummy content that shows exactly what the template is for.

Prompt: "Write realistic dummy content for this template — make it look like real, published content, not generic filler. Brand voice: [describe]. Template purpose: [describe]. I need: HEADLINE: [one example] SUBHEADLINE: [one example] BODY: [one example] CTA: [one example] Then write a second, completely different example so editors see the range." Paste Claude's dummy content into the template before sharing it as a blank
5

Write a usage guide for the template

A template without instructions gets misused within the first week. Ask Claude to write a short, plain-language checklist for anyone using the template.

Prompt: "Write a usage guide for this Canva template. It will be used by [marketing team / freelancers / social managers] who may have no design background. Template purpose: [describe] Fixed elements (never change): [list] Variable elements (change every use): [list] Most common mistakes people make: [describe or ask Claude to suggest them] Format: a numbered checklist, plain language, under 300 words. End with a 'before you publish' check." Add this guide as a notes page in Canva or link it in the template description
6

Publish as a Brand Template

Once the template is complete and tested with a real piece of content, publish it to your Brand Kit so the whole team has one-click access.

  • File → Share → Publish as Template
  • Set team access permissions
  • Name it clearly: e.g. "LinkedIn Product Update Post — [Brand]"
  • Paste the Claude-written usage guide into the template description
Brand Kit → Templates → anyone on the team can now find it and use it correctly

Part B — Building a Claude Skill for your template

A Claude Skill is a reusable prompt setup — saved as a Claude Project — that lets any team member generate perfectly on-brand, template-ready copy in a single message. Build it once, use it for every post.

1

Understand what a Skill does

A Skill is a pre-written system prompt (saved as a Claude Project) that tells Claude: your brand voice, the template's copy architecture, and the exact output format. Team members open the Project, type one sentence about what the post is about, and receive copy pre-formatted for every template field.

  • Lives in Claude.ai as a Project with instructions
  • Contains: brand voice, field specs, tone rules, output format, and avoid list
  • Result: anyone generates on-brand copy without design or copywriting experience
2

Compile everything Claude needs to know

Before writing the Skill, gather your inputs. You should already have most of this from Part A.

  • Brand voice description (from guidelines or from Chapter 02 of this guide)
  • Every template field: name, purpose, character limit, tone rule
  • Words and phrases to avoid: competitor terms, clichés, off-brand language
  • Two real examples of strong content for this template
  • One example of what bad content looks like (and why)
3

Ask Claude to write the Skill system prompt

Use Claude to draft the Skill itself — describe what it needs to do, paste your inputs, and ask for the system prompt. Then test and refine.

Prompt: "Help me write a Claude Skill (system prompt) for a Canva template content generator. The Skill should: 1. Set Claude up as an on-brand content writer for [brand name] 2. Know the brand voice: [paste voice description] 3. Know every template field and its rules: [paste copy architecture from Step 2 above] 4. Output content in labelled fields I can paste directly into Canva 5. Refuse to produce off-brand content and briefly explain why 6. End every output with a one-line self-check: 'Brand voice check: [yes/no — and why if no]' Also write a one-sentence user instruction to put at the top: tells team members exactly what to type to activate the Skill (e.g. 'Tell me the topic of this post and any key facts to include')." Output: a ready-to-use system prompt. Review it — test it yourself before sharing
4

Set up the Skill as a Claude Project

In Claude.ai, create a Project and paste your system prompt into the Project Instructions. Every conversation in this Project starts with the full Skill context — brand voice, field specs, all of it — loaded automatically.

  • claude.ai → New Project → Project Instructions → paste system prompt
  • Name clearly: e.g. "LinkedIn Post Generator — [Brand Name]"
  • Share the Project link with your team
  • Add the Canva template link in the Project description so it's one click away
Team members open the Project → type the topic → copy the output → open Canva → paste → done
5

Test the Skill across three scenarios

Before sharing with the team, run the Skill through three test inputs: a normal request, an edge case, and an off-brief request. All three should produce correct, on-brand output — or a clear explanation of why it can't.

Test inputs: 1. Normal: "Post about our new enterprise integration with Salesforce, launching next Tuesday" 2. Edge case: "Post about our office holiday closure — keep it short and warm" 3. Off-brief: "Write something edgy and provocative to cut through the noise this week" → Should flag if it conflicts with brand voice and offer a compliant alternative If the off-brief input produces off-brand content without flagging it, refine the system prompt and retest
6

Write the team one-pager

Document the workflow for anyone who's never used Claude before. Make it a single page — too long and nobody reads it.

Prompt: "Write a one-page team guide for this workflow: Step 1: Open the Claude Project [paste link] Step 2: Type your post topic and any key information in one message Step 3: Copy the labelled output (HEADLINE, SUBHEADLINE, BODY, CTA) Step 4: Open the Canva template [paste link] Step 5: Paste each field into the matching Canva text box Step 6: Swap the image, check the preview, publish Write for someone who has never used Claude. Include one worked example showing what a good input message looks like and what output it produces. Plain language. Under 400 words."

Full step-by-step walkthrough

1

Give Claude the full context first

Start a Claude conversation and share everything up front. The more context you give, the better the structure. Don't start typing slides until Claude has asked you questions.

Prompt: "I'm building a PowerPoint presentation. Here's everything: Audience: [who they are, what they know, what they care about] Goal: [what decision or action you need from them] Topic: [what the presentation covers] Constraints: [slide limit, time available, company template, topics to avoid] What I already have: [existing data, arguments, or structure] Ask me any questions you need before we start building." Let Claude ask questions — the answers unlock much better output than jumping straight to structure
2

Build the narrative arc before any slides

A good presentation has one clear throughline — every slide either advances it or it shouldn't be there. Map the story first, slides second.

Prompt: "Write a narrative arc for this presentation. Not a slide list — describe the journey: — What does the audience know and believe at the start? — What shift do you need to create, beat by beat? — What should they believe and be ready to do at the end? Then suggest a 3 or 4-part story structure. Name each part and explain what job it does." Example output: "Part 1: The cost of the current state. Part 2: Why this moment is different. Part 3: Our solution. Part 4: What happens next."
3

Generate the slide-by-slide outline

With the arc agreed, fill it with slides. At this stage you want titles and one-line premises only — evaluate the structure before writing any copy.

Prompt: "Using this arc, create a [N]-slide outline. For each slide: — Number and title (5 words max) — One-line premise: what this slide argues or shows — Slide type: title / data / quote / visual / text / transition No body copy yet — I want to approve the structure first." Tell Claude which slides to cut, combine, or reorder before you commit to content
4

Write copy section by section

With structure approved, write copy section by section — not all slides at once. Review each section before moving on so you can adjust tone as you go.

Prompt: "Write copy for slides [1–4] — Section 1: [section name]. For each slide: TITLE: [exact slide title] HEADLINE: the single statement this slide makes — max 10 words BODY: max 3 bullet points or 2 short sentences — supporting evidence only SPEAKER NOTE: what to say aloud — 30–45 seconds, written naturally, not as bullet points Tone: [describe — e.g. "confident but not aggressive, data-backed, no hype"] Audience: [remind Claude who's in the room]" Speaker notes are the most underused PowerPoint element — build them from the start, not as an afterthought
5

Cut slides with Claude's help

First-draft decks are almost always 30% too long. Paste the full outline back into Claude and ask for the editing pass.

Prompt: "Here is my complete slide outline: [paste all titles and premises] Which 3 slides are weakest — because they duplicate another, don't advance the narrative, or could be merged? For each: — What is it doing wrong? — What should I do with it: cut / merge with [slide X] / rewrite to do a different job?" Most presentations benefit from cutting 3–5 slides. Claude will find them faster than you will.
6

Prepare for the room

Before you present, ask Claude to simulate the audience — their reactions, their likely questions, and the objection you're least prepared for.

Prompt: "I'm presenting this deck to [describe audience precisely — their role, what they know, what they're nervous about]. For each of these likely challenges: — They're skeptical of [specific claim in the deck] — They'll ask about [something you haven't fully addressed] — They'll push back on [your main recommendation] Give me: the likely question or objection, the underlying concern (what they're really asking), and a 2-sentence in-the-room response."

Slide-level prompts

Rewrite an overloaded slide
This slide has too much on it. Rewrite it so it makes exactly one point. Current content: [paste slide title and all bullet points] The one point it needs to make: [if you know it — or ask Claude to identify it] Audience: [who is reading this] Output: new title (max 6 words), one headline statement (max 12 words), max 2 supporting bullets.

Why it works: Forcing Claude to identify "the one point" clarifies your own thinking. The most common slide failure is trying to make three points at once.

Write a data slide with insight-led title
I have a data slide: [describe the chart and what the data shows] The point I want this data to make: [the so-what — not the topic, the conclusion] Write: — Slide title that states the conclusion (not the topic) — max 8 words — One-sentence insight the audience takes away — Speaker note: explain the data in plain language — what it shows, why it matters, what comes next

Why it works: Data slides fail when the title describes the chart ("Q3 Revenue by Region") instead of the insight ("North outperforms for the third quarter running"). Claude will always push toward insight-led titles.

Write a strong opening slide
Write the opening slide for a presentation about: [topic] Audience: [describe] The tension I want to open with: [the uncomfortable truth or provocative question that frames everything] Output: — A headline that creates immediate tension or curiosity (max 10 words) — A subheadline that expands it (max 15 words) — Speaker note: how to say this opening out loud so it lands in the room

Curate — don't collect

Every skill adds to Claude's context window and can slow down responses. Install only what you use in a given week.

  • Week 1: frontend-design + brand-guidelines + one document skill. That's it.
  • Week 2: Add the Figma or Canva connector skill based on your primary tool
  • Week 3: Add one community skill targeting a specific pain point
  • After every 5 conversations: Remove anything you haven't used once

Skills — what to watch out for

  • Prompt injection risk: Only install skills from Anthropic's repo or sources you've personally verified. A malicious SKILL.md can contain instructions that override your conversation.
  • Context window creep: Too many installed skills slow responses and cause conflicts. Start with 3–5 maximum.
  • Install commands change: Verify the exact command with the source before running. Repo slugs sometimes change after updates.
  • Skills are not memory: They provide instructions, not persistent data. Your brand colors won't carry across sessions unless they're in the skill file itself or in a Project knowledge base.

Skill anatomy — what every SKILL.md needs

A skill is just a folder with a SKILL.md file at the root. Claude reads the frontmatter to decide when to load it, and the body for how to execute. Optional references/, scripts/, and assets/ directories hold supporting files.

FieldRequiredPurpose
nameYesLowercase, hyphens, max 64 chars. Acts as the skill's filename and identifier.
descriptionYesHighest-leverage line. Describes what the skill does AND when Claude should reach for it. Drives retrieval.
Body (markdown)YesConcise instructions, examples, output format rules. Keep under ~5K tokens; split into references/ for anything heavier.
references/NoLong docs loaded on demand. Linked from the body via knowledge:// or relative paths.
scripts/NoExecutable helpers (.sh, .py, .ts). Copied then run — useful for asset transforms, exports, validators.
assets/NoFixed files Claude reads but does not execute — templates, fonts, brand tokens, fixture data.

Team governance — 8 rules

Checklist

Running skills across a design team

  • 1.One skill, one job. Split anything that does two things — composability beats megaskills.
  • 2.Description carries the load. Write description like a search query — what it does AND when to use it. Misses = skill never runs.
  • 3.SKILL.md under 5K tokens. Push long material into references/ — loaded on demand.
  • 4.Workspace vs. personal. Anything brand-related → workspace, so everyone sees it.
  • 5.Test the trigger. Paraphrase the task three ways before publishing; if the skill misses, rewrite the description.
  • 6.Tokens in assets/, not SKILL.md. Updating a colour shouldn't require rewriting the skill.
  • 7.Skills aren't memory. For state across sessions, use a Project knowledge base.
  • 8.Audit quarterly. Design systems change, links rot, conventions drift.

Adobe scripting

illustrator-mcp — Real Illustrator Control via Claude

Community-built MCP server by krVatsal. Controls Adobe Illustrator directly from Claude Desktop or Claude Code using ExtendScript (.jsx) — the same scripting engine Illustrator has used for decades. Works on Windows via COM and macOS via AppleScript.

What this unlocks that the Adobe connector can't do: Real layer manipulation, artboard control, path operations, text on a path, symbol management, export to any format Illustrator supports. Claude writes the .jsx script, the MCP server runs it in your open Illustrator document, and the result appears immediately on your artboard. No Express tier limitations.

Project instructions

How to Write Effective Claude Project Instructions

Claude Projects are the team-scaling layer — a workspace where every conversation inherits your brand context, style guide rules, and client-specific constraints without you repeating them. The quality of your Project instructions determines the quality of every output your team produces.

What makes a Project instruction work (from J.D. Hodges' validated template, jdhodges.com):

  • —What this project is: one sentence on the purpose (e.g. "This is the brand design workspace for [Client]. All outputs are client deliverables.")
  • —What Claude should always do: use brand voice, match the colour tokens, refer to the uploaded style guide
  • —What Claude should never do: use competitor names, create new colour values not in the palette, generate copy in first-person unless specified
  • —Format rules: output type preferences, heading styles, file format defaults
  • —Key contacts / reference docs: link to uploaded brand guidelines, component library, tone-of-voice doc

Limit: ~8,000 characters (~2,000 words). Keep it tight — short directive rules outperform long prose. Iterate after 3–5 real conversations and remove anything Claude isn't using.

Important caveats for 2026

Read before you invest time in any of these tools

  • Claude Design is token-metered on a separate quota. It runs on Anthropic Labs' Claude Opus 4.7 and has its own weekly token allowance separate from your regular Claude and Claude Code usage. Heavy iteration burns through it quickly — community reports suggest 2–3 complex design sessions can use a significant portion of the weekly quota. Use it for first-draft exploration and visual direction; finish production work in Figma or Canva.
  • The Adobe connector operates at Express tier in practice. Despite being marketed as covering Photoshop, Premiere, and Illustrator, independent testing found meaningful gaps between the marketing and the actual capability. For any production-level retouching or video editing, use the apps directly. The connector excels at Express-tier batch work — social asset variants, background removal, basic adjustments — and that's genuinely useful.
  • Figma's code↔canvas roundtrip has known limitations. Business logic, component bindings, and state don't survive the trip in either direction. The generate_figma_design tool (free in beta) had rollout and availability bugs at launch. If it's not working, disconnect competing connectors and plugins, restart Figma, and try again. The html.to.design MCP is a more reliable alternative for the code→canvas direction.
  • Skills share your context window — install fewer, not more. Every active skill adds tokens to every message. More than 6–8 skills and you'll see slower responses and increased conflicts. Start with 3–5 targeted ones; remove anything unused after a week.
  • Only install skills from trusted sources. A malicious SKILL.md can contain instructions that override your conversation or exfiltrate data. Before installing any community skill: check the GitHub repo has recent commits, read the actual SKILL.md file before running the install command, and verify the install command matches the current repo name (slugs change).
  • Typo-squat warning: Multiple repositories falsely claim to be "Claude Design download" or "Claude Design installer." The real Claude Design runs in your browser at claude.ai/design — there is no desktop app, no installer, no download. Any site or repo claiming otherwise is not affiliated with Anthropic.

IP, ownership & client work

Who owns AI-assisted work, what's copyrightable, and what belongs in your client contracts — the questions every working designer hits within a month, answered plainly.

The six questions, answered

QuestionShort answerWhat to actually do
Who owns Claude's outputs? Anthropic's terms assign its rights in outputs to you — you can use them commercially. If you're on a Team/Enterprise plan, confirm nothing in your org's agreement changes this. Keep a copy of the terms version you worked under.
Is AI-assisted work copyrightable? Purely machine-generated material isn't, per the US Copyright Office — but your human creative contribution (selection, arrangement, substantial modification) can be protected. Keep your iteration history: briefs, revisions, the work you did on top. It's your evidence of human authorship if it ever matters.
Will client data train the model? Business plans (Team, Enterprise, API) aren't used for training by default. Consumer plans have a training preference you control in settings. For NDA work: use a business plan, or verify Settings → Privacy on your account before pasting anything sensitive.
Do I have to tell clients I use AI? Few laws require it for design work (as of 2026), but client contracts and procurement policies increasingly do. Disclose proactively in your SOW. Being asked later and having no answer is the real reputational risk.
Are names Claude generates safe to use? Generation isn't clearance — the same name may already be in commerce or registered. Trademark-search every shortlisted name before presenting it (the Naming chapter includes a screening prompt).
Generated imagery in deliverables? Ownership and indemnification vary by generator and by subscription tier. Prefer commercially-positioned tools for final assets, keep generation receipts, and read the current terms of the tool that rendered the file.

The 4-line AI clause designers actually use

A workable SOW pattern: (1) AI tools may be used and will be disclosed on request. (2) Every deliverable receives full human review and creative direction. (3) No client confidential material enters consumer AI accounts. (4) Final IP in deliverables is assigned per this contract. Adapt the language with your attorney.

Read this box before relying on this chapter

  • This is orientation, not legal advice. Copyright and AI law differ by country and are moving fast — rulings and guidance have shifted every year since 2023.
  • For contract language, talk to an IP attorney. The clause pattern above is a starting structure, not a substitute.
  • Terms change. Re-check Anthropic's and your image generator's terms when a big client project starts, not after it ships.

Writing better prompts

The output is only as good as the input. These principles apply everywhere — whether you're naming a brand, writing a brief, or asking for a critique.

Give Claude a role

"Act as a senior CD" or "Think like a skeptical client" changes the perspective — and the quality — of the output dramatically.

Name what to avoid

The most underused field in any prompt. "Avoid words like journey, authentic, passionate" eliminates the generic before Claude starts.

Ask for structure

Requesting a specific format — categories, columns, before/after — forces range and prevents Claude from defaulting to similar-sounding options.

Iterate, don't restart

Your first prompt is a starting point. Say "that's too safe — push further" or "keep this direction but make it less corporate." Build on what's good.

Share context once

Paste your brief or key brand truths at the start and say "keep this in mind throughout." You won't need to re-explain yourself in every message.

Ask for less, then expand

3 strong ideas beats 20 mediocre ones. Ask for fewer options and request that each one be fully thought through, not just named.
